How Brexit Could Endanger Research Funding

When the UK breaks away from the European Union next March, academics worry it may be leaving important research funding opportunities behind.

The £62bn EU research initiative, Horizon2020, has proved lucrative for UK universities, which have nabbed roughly £8bn of funding since 2014, or 28 percent of the funding awarded to higher education. 

“Currently the situation is very good for UK researchers because they can apply for these EU grants,” said Ludovic Highman, a senior research associate at UCL. “What’s not certain at all is the next seven year cycle, and that’s really the most important thing,” he added, referring to the block of EU funding starting after the official Brexit date.

Just months after the Brexit vote, Philip Hammond said the government would guarantee all funding grants awarded before the UK leaves.

But whether or not the UK’s separation from the EU will affect its future access to funding — and its ability to do research — remains uncertain. For some, though, the answer is clear.

“If you pull the plug on a line of funding, which you’re bound to do with Brexit, you’re bound to do less,” said John Dainton, professor emeritus of physics at the University of Liverpool and spokesman for the Council for the Defence of British Universities. “Simple as that.”

Show me the money

If past funding is any indication, Britain’s top universities have the most to lose. Between 2014 and September 2017, Oxford and Cambridge together pulled in £4.5bn in EU research grants, covering over 650 participants. UCL and Imperial College of London were award £2.1bn and £1.5bn, respectively.

Losing funding may impact smaller universities more, said Highman, especially if they receive proportionately more EU grants than larger ones and cannot rely on their size to make up for the lost funding. 

But the sector as a whole will nevertheless feel the loss of EU money.

“What it does is take the infrastructure base for funding and the money base for funding projects back to the levels of the 1970s when we were always struggling to keep up with the rest of the world,” said Dainton.

“It is certainly crippling to some extent British science,” he added. “It can’t compete on a level playing field with France, Germany, Italy and other European countries.”

Funding for research also fuels the UK economy, according to a 2016 report by Universities UK, which represents the country’s higher education institutions. EU research funding adds £1bn to GDP, half of which comes from outside the university sector, and accounts for 19,000 jobs across the country, the report found.

Participation in the funding programs has also not put a burden on the Treasury. Despite contributing £5.4bn to the last seven-year funding cycle, Britain got back roughly £8.8bn.

Publish or perish

Academics have a saying when it comes to research: “Publish or perish.” Journal publications — or the lack thereof — can make or break careers, and one of the key metrics for judging a publication’s success is the number of times other academics cite it. Research shows that papers with international co-authors receive more citations, which is bad news for post-Brexit academia if the UK’s departure means less collaboration.

“You might see that kind of highly cited research output diminish, and that would then in consequence diminish UK universities’ good standing in world rankings,” said Higham

Some researchers aren’t sticking around to find out. “Already we’ve seen the top scientists who came in the last five or six year — an awful lot of them have left the UK,” Dainton said.

Alister Scott of Northumbria University took to Twitter last month to lament one of his colleagues leaving the UK because of possible funding losses. “We cant afford to lose such talented people from our universities,” he wrote.

According to Dainton, European researchers who have collaborated with academics in the UK have been reluctant to let their British colleagues lead projects for fear that it will limit future funding opportunities. The greater loss is one of collaboration and opportunity, he said.

“I would certainly say that among the world of physics, whatever sort of physicist you are whatever sort of engineer you are, everybody sees it as a huge negative,” said Dainton.

Uncertain times

Like many other sectors readying for the impact of Brexit, the source of future research funding remains uncertain. Even with the government’s commitment to fund grants awarded before March 2019, projects initiated after the date may struggle for funding.

“It’s not very clear where the UK government is going to get that extra funding from especially once you have to deal with all other areas when exiting the EU. There’ll be so many competing priorities,” said Higham.

It’s another piece of “Brexit confusion,” according to Jonathan Rokem of UCL who tweeted about the question of research funding.

The UK could still retain access to the next seven-year funding program, but that participation could come with a fee and with limited rights. 

“It is likely that we would be able to apply as an associate country at the least and as a major participant of previous frameworks there is a will to work with the UK on maintaining access,” Richard Wigley of the grant application consultancy GrantTree said in an email. 

“At the moment our advice is to apply while it is still an available funding stream,” Wigley said.
