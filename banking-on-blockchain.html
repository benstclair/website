Banking on blockchain

The technology associated with cryptocurrencies looks very different to the banks adopting it

When Adam Rizvi tried to make a Bitcoin transaction in December, Barclays froze his accounts. He wasn’t able to use them for another 10 days. “No-one (Not the bank manager, bank staff, telephone support team or fraud team) would talk to me about it,” he said in an email.

Since then, Rizvi says he’s used another UK bank to make cryptocurrency transactions and through his company, Crypto Bates Group, recommends that his clients do the same.

Even as Barclays very publicly experiments with blockchain technology, which underlies many cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin, its position towards the digital currencies has remained muted. In March the bank allowed a UK subsidiary of Coinbase, a San Fransisco-based cryptocurrency exchange, to open an account with its own subsidiary.

But the connection is more “arm’s length” than anything, according to Robert Olsen, chief operating officer of the UK-based, blockchain-like company Radix. “The minute you say blockchain, the banks are like ‘ahh,’” he said, later adding he plans to use a Luxembourg-based bank to open an account.

When reached for comment, a spokesperson for Barclays referred to Vice Chairman Jeremy Wilson’s comments in February 2017 as the company’s “public position on blockchain”.

“I don’t think I’ve ever heard it mentioned in Barclays or in the Whitechapel Think Tank that blockchain is anything other than a significant opportunity for the banks. And not just for the incumbents, but for everyone,” Wilson said at the time.

Others, however, criticise the banking industry’s current use of the technology.

“Banks hate blockchains, they'll never adopt them. They want a blockchain that isn't a blockchain,” Matthew Di Ferrante, founder at Zero Knowledge Labs, said in an email.

Anja Bedford, who helps lead Deutsche Bank’s blockchain efforts, calls the criticism “unheeded”, pointing to the precautions banks are required to take. A breach in a bank similar to those seen in cryptocurrency exchanges would mean “that bank is finished,” Bedford said. “We have to safe keep your data, and we have to comply with regulation,” she added.

Deutsche Bank, too, has been experimenting with blockchain technology. Like Barclays’ bifurcated stance on blockchain and cryptocurrencies — adopting and experimenting with the technology internally while distancing itself from other aspects — their position reflects the challenges many banks face balancing the existing regulatory framework and the desire to innovate with a technology that eschews centralisation and identification.

The challenges mean that when banks talk about using blockchain, they’re referring to a different type of blockchain than what is commonly understood, which has been defined largely by impressions of the Bitcoin network. To more blockchain purists like Di Ferrante, the changes may negate blockchain’s innovations.

But many banks and financial services firms remain optimistic about their use of blockchain and maintain that it will have a transformative impact on the industry.

“If there's going to be an innovation or revolution in banking services, I'm sure that the banks will be ahead of it, and we will just change the way we serve customers,” said Steven Wang of RBS.

The question remains how that changed industry will look.

Misplaced innovators?

Banks aren’t necessarily known as chief innovators, tending instead to supply funding to or buy up those that do the work. With blockchain potentially threatening the financial industry, though, banks have attempted to adapt the technology to their business.

Blockchain, also known as distributed ledger technology or simply DLT, is difficult to define. While still a nascent development, the community remains fraught with disagreements over what, exactly, constitutes a proper use of the technology.

At its core, though, blockchain is a database structure distributed over various locations. Its entries are cryptographically secured and linked to previous transactions, forming a mostly immutable chain. Depending on its design, all users can edit and write to the blockchain, or receive permission to do so, and the system includes a method for users to come to an agreement about the legitimate state of the ledger.

To the technology’s evangelicals, blockchain is set to change everything from supply chain management and cross-border payments, to medical record storage and personal identification. But some doubt banks are positioned well to lead these innovations, especially compared to newer startups.

“I think if we see it take off, we're going to see it take off in something lower risk and something which is inherently more innovative,” said Eitan Jankelewitz, a partner at Sheridans, a London-based law firm. “Financial services are neither of those things,” he added.

Michael Zargham, CEO and founder of California-based BlockScience, also doubts that banks will be the testing ground for revolutionary blockchain developments. But, he thinks, they have come to understand the technology and attempted to apply it to their work.

“Banks are going to do bank things,” he said. “That's fine, but it's a form of backward innovation.”

When it comes to “bank things” and making an impact on the financial industry, the traditional firms might have an advantage, especially when it comes to navigating existing regulation.

“I'm not sure whether this is the best place, but I would think that this is a safe place to do innovation,” said Wang.

“They [the banks] have a history of knowing how to keep technology safe in terms of privacy issues, in terms of customer identity, in terms of keeping within the limits of legal and ethical constraints,” he added.

Banking solutions

If anything, blockchain developments may have forced financial institutions to re-evaluate their present data systems. But blockchain technologies do not necessarily work for all information stored in databases, said Bedford.

Regardless, a rethink involves a significant departure from the blockchain designs made popular by cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin and Ethereum’s ether. These systems’ appeal lies in their ability to democratise information and mask users’ identities. In effect, cryptocurrency blockchains bring trust into trustless networks, suppling a framework for strangers to be confident in the validity of the ledger.

Neither attribute, publicity or full anonymity (or pseudonymity in the case of networks that identify users by a unique hash) makes sense for banks. With know-your-customer (KYC) and anti-money-laundering (AML) regulations in place, banks are held accountable for the businesses and individuals they serve.

As a result, their blockchains — if you call them that — will come with significant adjustments.

“I think if you are one of the legacy banks you probably cannot use the decentralised model without falling out of compliance with other things, and this is a real cost to them,” said David Yermack, who chairs the Finance Department at New York University.

“Industry is gravitating toward a model where there would be a gatekeeper who would be the monopolist custodian who would have the exclusive right to update the blockchain,” Yermack added.

Corda has been this model and the industry’s solution to its regulatory needs. Developed by R3, which leads a consortium of 200 banks and financial institutions, Corda is a permission-based, blockchain-like protocol developed for business.

“Corda was built by the financial industry, for the financial industry,” write Charles Cooper and Isabelle Corbet in testimony R3 submitted to parliament in May.

The system, which R3 have said is not, strictly speaking, a blockchain, functions as a shared ledger where transactions are visible only to the parties involved. There’s no mining, currency, or broad consensus mechanism outside of the agreement of the parties engaging in a transaction.

“If we wanted to be skeptical, we might say that Corda’s team was set an impossible task – to design a flavor of blockchain that would suit the banks funding R3,” writes Gideon Greenspan, founder and CEO of UK-based Coin Sciences.

In some ways, Greenspan writes, Corda can be seen “as a heroic but ultimately unsatisfactory compromise between the desire of R3’s members to do something blockchainy, and the commercial and regulatory constraints under which they exist.”

Even if a blockchain ends up meeting the visions enthusiasts have predicted, banks will have to do it their own way.

“It will be an iterative process,” said Bedford. Banks will move up to the more innovative applications as they become more feasible.

Bedford said banks have some blockchain-like systems running alongside legacy systems, but are unable to replace the current methods in some cases because of rules mandating that certain securities be issued in paper form and physically held by a central securities depository.

“Some of the other banks, especially JP Morgan, they already have it running, but most of us are kind of in this shadow production phase where basically the system is running parallel because again, too, regulation,” said Bedford.

Major banks have realised, she added, that their current interconnectivity means any blockchain-like solution will involve significant collaboration. Consortia like R3 then become key.

Waiting for a changed industry

When it comes to actually instituting blockchain or blockchain-inspired technologies, organisations require a technological and personnel infrastructure capable of maintaining and debugging the network. Jankelewitz doubts that the pool of talented developers is currently sufficient to meet these needs for banks.

“They're not going to swap onto that anytime soon unless they're totally assured that there is the resourcing there to make it work,” he said. “If it can't serve five million people from day one, then it's not going to work for them.”

Regardless of whether or not banks end up instituting “proper” blockchains, the true impact of the technology, or inspired technologies, remains unclear.

“It would be too restrictive to think that the blockchain stuff would be able to change completely entirely the banking processes — or some of the banking processes,” said Paolo Tasca, executive director at the UCL Centre for Blockchain Technologies in London.

“But together with other toolbox like big data technology, machine learning, artificial intelligence and so on, then it could change a lot.”

So what does that mean for the future of banking? Martin Walker of the Centre for Evidence-Based Management remains skeptical that blockchain will actually increase efficiency and see widespread adoption. After all, it’s not yet possible to automate every aspect of the financial industry.

“Simply having a blockchain does not actually get people to update the status of where the payment is,” Walker told Parliament in May.

The critique underscores some of the challenges the industry faces before blockchain-inspired technologies can become widely used — if they do at all. Interoperability, or connecting different blockchain systems together, also poses a challenge for an industry accustomed to keeping its ledgers separate.

Tasca envisions a future in which banks become more of a “data warehouse” where external providers access the information they need about their customers while banks serve more nuanced purposes.

“The banks become simply a tool that allow more flexible, more quick, and less heavyweight operators to offer specific service that serve a specific segment of the market,” Tasca said.

And yet others have been more bullish on the technology’s transformative impact on the financial industry.

Yermack, who considers blockchain to be the most significant innovation since double entry bookkeeping, predicts that retail banking operations will shift away from the industry leaders. The banks, in his view, will disappear slowly, much like the old US airline industry consolidated and changed in the 20th century.

The disruptive blockchain revolution in banking may be a long way off, though. In June the blockchain-like company Ripple said that at the moment, blockchain’s scalability and privacy issues make it unlikely banks will adopt the technology for cross border transactions, one of the more talked about financial applications.

Over the next year Bedford predicts that the industry will continue to see the right, and wrong, business cases for blockchain. Or make the necessary developments.

“You still see this hype with lots of things happening,” said Bedford. “I personally think we will hit this value of disillusionment on the hype cycle, but that's when you see the actual good use cases happening.”
