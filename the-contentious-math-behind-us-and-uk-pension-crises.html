The contentious math behind US and UK pension “crises”

Funding gaps have defined how pension funds and lawmakers behave — for better or worse

As academics from universities across the UK continue to walk out of classrooms and protest  proposed changes to their pension plans, they share what has become a trans-Atlantic concern about the future of retirement schemes.

But their worry has little to do with the so-called funding ratio that shows the critical underfunding of US public pension plans and the Universities Superannuation Scheme (USS) for UK academics. It has to do with the accounting behind that number.

“Although the funding ratio is the most popular and recognised metric of a pension plan’s condition, it is not by itself a reliable indicator of the plan’s condition,” Alex Brown, a researcher with the US-based National Association of State Retirement Administrators, said in an email. “Rather, the funding ratio is a ‘point in time’ measurement of a pension plan’s progress toward meeting its long-term funding objectives.”

The funding ratio compares present asset values to the amount plans anticipate owing retirees in the future. If, in 25 years, a plan expects to pay out $1bn and accounts for a 7.8% annual return, the typical expectation held over the last 16 years by US public pension funds, it would need roughly $150m in assets today to be considered fully funded (assuming it received no further contributions). 

It’s this process of judging a fund’s current health by its ability to meet all future payouts that draws concerns with pensioners. It assumes, they argue, that the fund must be able to continue operating even if the beneficiaries’ business has collapsed. For UK academics, it would mean that UK universities have ceased to exist. 

“USS has the advantage that it doesn’t really need to act like that because there’s pretty much zero chance of the whole sector disappearing,” Sam Marsh of the University of Sheffield said in an interview.

Public pension holders in the US, whose employers include state-wide education systems and city police forces, would agree.

“Under the right conditions, a pension system can pay benefits indefinitely at funding levels much lower than ‘full’ funding,” writes Tom Sgouros of the US-based Haas Institute for a Fair and Inclusive Society. 

The data support this claim. Contributions and investment earnings in US schemes and the USS have largely exceeded benefit payments over the last decade.

But policymakers have seen the fundings gaps as reasons for concern and attempted to close the holes. US states and cities have turned to alternative investments like private equity, hedge funds, commodities and real estate to boost returns — roughly doubling the median plan’s exposure to these assets over the last decade, according to an analysis of data from 170 state and city plans.

The USS has responded by proposing to shift all future plans to a defined contribution model, where the amount received in retirement depends on the fund’s investment returns. Educators say the proposal would leave them worse off in retirement.

But both sides can agree on at least one point: It’s all about the accounting.
