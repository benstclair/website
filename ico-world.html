ICO World: Will the hip new fundraising model get over its regulatory hurdle?

While crypto and blockchain evangelicals foresee an end to venture capital, others remain skeptical of the new fundraising model

It’s a mad, mad ICO world. Or at least it used to be.

As regulators around the world continue to weigh in on initial coin offerings — the sale of digital tokens as a way for companies to raise money — many decry the attempted government interference in an idea that defies centralisation. Others welcome what they see as a much-needed push to standardise an otherwise beneficial process so far defined by its fraudulent actors.

Whatever the reality, ICOs have proven to be a lucrative form of financing. According to an analysis of funding rounds listed on Crunchbase, a database of global investments, ICOs raised $9.3 billion in 335 global offerings between January 2017 and mid-April 2018.

Other sources put the figure much higher, including a study by Coinbase estimating that offerings in 2018 have already pulled in more that $8 billion.

ICOs are particularly hard to quantify without regulatory filings and consistently reliable reports, but the trend over the last year has been clear.

Crunchbase data shows that there were only three ICOs in 2016, compared to 220 in 2017 and 105 in the first three and a half of months of 2018. Globally since the start of last year, the funding model has attracted the interest of companies in 43 countries.

The question is, are ICOs are here to stay?

Security or utility

Unlike shares that give investors a stake in a company’s profits, tokens offer the potential for future use on a company’s platform. The exact nature of offerings vary widely. Some companies offer tokens long before they have a product to show for it while others like the messaging app Telegram do so after developing an established tool. But at their most basic, coin offerings are one of many ways companies can raise money — and do so without the disclosures common in the more traditional venture capital model.

Once they’re offered, tokens often trade hands on the secondary market like many other investments

Those who argue ICOs are separate from securities see them more as donations or buying “utility” in a company’s future product, not an interest in the company itself.

It’s the regulators’ assessments that matter, though. The Securities and Exchange Commission, a US regulator, broadly defines a security as an asset where a third party induces an expectation of future return.

Their stance on ICOs is more conditional, stressing that a token’s use and purpose, not what it’s called, determines whether or not it represents a security. In June the regulator concluded that Bitcoin and Ether, trading on a secondary market and under certain conditions, may not constitute a security. But wrapped up in an investment package, both cryptocurrencies would become securities and fall under existent regulation.

Eitan Jankelewitz, a partner at Sheridans, a London-based law firm, argues ICOs must comply with regulations. He said Sheridans has advised over two-dozen potential coin offerings, but only seen a handful through the offering itself.

“You have to assume that any ICO token is intended to be freely transferable,” said Jankelewitz. “Therefore laws that would ordinarily apply to things like public markets, stock exchanges and things — they begin to bite because you have an interest in a company which can be an exchange between different individuals.”

The regulations help guard against fraudulent offerings, which deploy similar strategies as any other investment scheme, Jankelewitz said.

But fraudulent ICOs are not hard to spot, according to David Yermack, chair of the finance department at New York University.

The legitimate ICOs disclose a good amount of information about their business, he said, relaying his findings as part of an upcoming paper, in order “to signal to the market that they have a good business plan, a good track record”.

Yermack questions whether regulators like the SEC should have to police coin offerings at all, but the SEC’s ongoing position suggests they see the possibility of a future role.

How palatable coin offerings become to regulators may decide whether the ICO money machine keeps rolling, according to Philip Young, marketing director for the Gibraltar Stock Exchange, which also provides a venue to trade tokens. At a small London blockchain event in May, Young told his audience that the sector’s innovation needed to extend beyond the technology.

“You’ve got to think about ethical innovation as well,” he said. “Really creating standards for us as a community to grow up and create a better marketplace — to change it from what it is at the moment, very much a niche part of the economy, to something that should become mainstream.”
