Navigating the blockchain jungle

Defining blockchain technology is less important than matching its various designs to real business needs

Blockchain’s biggest threat sits in a one-room office in a London co-working space. Or so they would have you believe. The company, Radix, has yet to launch publicly and has only the statistics from internal tests to demonstrate their innovation. But they claim their product will reinvent the technology landscape: “Blockchain, your days are numbered,” reads the slogan visible on a large display board in their office.

Radix, which draws its name from the Latin for “root”, is in many ways indistinguishable from other technology startups. They have a sharp-looking website with links to whitepapers explaining their product design. They operate a channel on the messaging app Telegram to promote their work and answer questions from potential future users. Their code-obsessed founder is controversial, and some argue the company is nothing more than a scam. They may have potential, but they’re entirely unproven.

In other words, they’re the typical blockchain company — just don’t call them that. Radix’s chief operating officer, Robert Olsen, says they don’t have a blockchain. They don’t mine currency, require obscene amounts of energy to operate, or even use blocks. “What we bring to the table is massive scalability, stability and sustainability,” said Olsen, rattling off Radix’s features like a sales pitch he’s given dozens of times.  

As other companies work to assert their connection to blockchain technology and benefit from the attention, Radix has looked to separate itself from the term. Their claims raise the question of what it means to be a blockchain company and to utilise the innovation. 

“To this day there is no accepted definition of what a blockchain is. There is no standard,” according to Anja Bedford, who helps lead Deutsche Bank’s blockchain efforts.

Indeed, the line between blockchain, a distributed digital ledger, and a traditional database is hazy at best, and many definitions rely on the blockchain design used to support Bitcoin, which has few applications outside the cryptocurrency. 

The continuum between new blockchain technology and its alternatives is further complicated by the fact that most, if not all, of the technology that makes up a blockchain design was created decades earlier.

“As people have tried to take that technology and apply it, it has mutated incredibly,” Martin Walker of the Centre for Evidence-Based Management told Parliament in May. “It is almost meaningless to talk about blockchain as a single technology, because it is not anymore.”

The resulting fragmentation of blockchain designs poses challenges not only for those trying to understand it but also for those looking to incorporate it into their business, especially when they see blockchain as the ultimate fix-all solution, Walker said.

How organisations define blockchain becomes less important than figuring out how they plan to implement it and the problems they’re looking to solve.

Using the technology correctly can help companies manage data more efficiently, but misusing it can create unnecessary complexity. “It's a tool and you either use it properly or you don’t,” said Michael Zargham, founder and chief executive of California-based BlockScience. “And like any other tool, you can always invent an example of a thing that doesn't make sense.”

But, Zargham said, “that doesn’t mean it wont change the world”.

Genesis block

The original blockchain was used to support the cryptocurrency Bitcoin by keeping track of transactions bewteen users and mark ownership of each coin. To do so, transactions are listed on digital pages (blocks) that some users (miners) validate roughly every 10 minutes by solving computational problems. The user who solves the problem the fastest submits their solution to the network, which in turn comes to a consensus to verify the miner’s work (proof of work) and adds the block to the larger chain. Each block references the one before it — all the way back to the original, genesis block — so changing the chain becomes increasingly more challenging as the ledger grows.

The Bitcoin blockchain supports a self sustaining network, as the currency gets created and rewarded to miners when they add blocks, so basing a definition of blockchain on its first use can be limiting.

Instead, operators have broken the Bitcoin blockchain into its components, listing those as defining blockchain features.

“You don't need a chain, you don't need to have specific blocks, or you don’t really need to have a proof of work or any coins or tokens in that case,” according to Steven Wang, who works on blockchain research at RBS. “The minimum requirement is the network really, a decentralised network.”

Wang’s definition relates to the distributed aspect of the disturbed ledger. Unlike more traditional database designs that locate the authoritative copy in a central location, the Bitcoin blockchain distributes the records to all nodes in the network so each holds a complete copy. Proponents argue decentralisation makes the network more secure.

But according to Zargham, full public distribution is irrelevant since blockchains can exist within  organisations. His blockchain definition includes a system of rules for changing and agreeing on the official state of the ledger — the consensus mechanism and the system’s governance structure.

Bitcoin’s blockchain requires a majority of nodes to agree on the valid ledger each time a miner proposes the next block. Because access to the Bitcoin network is permissionless, anyone can open a node and download the blockchain. The governance design allows for so-called trustless actors, those who have never met or may have competing interests, to come to an agreement on the state of the ledger. 

At its most basic, blockchain remains a data structure, a way of storing information much like the commonly used SQL or JSON designs. 

“It's more in that way down in the weeds tier of technology,” said Zargham, who compares blockchain to a motor controller in mechanical devices.

Paolo Tasca, executive director at the UCL Centre for Blockchain Technologies in London, agrees, referring to blockchain as “a foundational technology”.

“It will have an impact on the architectural infrastructures of our systems that we are using,” he said. 

Blockchain jungle

The foundational aspect of the technology — and its amorphous definition — means that the design landscape has fragmented into various blockchain and alternative blockchain designs. Tasca calls this environment “a jungle of different blockchain architecture”.

The variations mean that a blockchain’s specifications must be paired with a specific business system and wont simply solve problems across the board.

“At the end of the day blockchain is just a technology, and you've got to figure out with smart contracts how you can actually apply it and what the business logic is behind it,” said Bedford, referring to the automated agreements run in conjunction with a blockchain.

While Bitcoin continues to run on the same model, other blockchains have shifted away from its proof-of-work verification because of its slow speed and high electricity requirements, globally estimated to consume more energy per year than Switzerland.

Tendermint and Ethereum’s Casper utilise a proof-of-stake model in which miners are determined by the size of their coin holdings. The idea is that those with the largest stake in the system will be less likely to corrupt it.

However, a proof-of-stake fault control risks concentrating mining with a few users, who, depending on how the network is constructed, could remain anonymous. 

Proof-of-authority, on the other hand, could identify those central users who may exert more control over who has access to the network. 

In R3’s Corda, which was developed specifically for use in the financial industry, an authority node grants others permission to read and write into the blockchain, and only those nodes participating in a transaction are aware of the details. The governance model eschews the Bitcoin dream of disintermediation, but proves more suitable for banks and other organisations looking to protect their data and comply with regulations. 

The question then is the degree of centralisation that disqualifies a network design from being considered a blockchain — or if that distinction even matters.

In these more centralised models, the need for a currency to incentivise nodes to participate becomes less relevant, especially if all users have a non-financial reason for being involved — either because they all belong to the same organisation or have an interest in jointly using a blockchain. 

Thus as the organisations move away from a Bitcoin-styled network, the system starts to look less and less like the original blockchain. 

Bedford considers these centralised designs to be more akin to distributed ledgers than actual blockchains. Choosing the right centralisation model depends on the business problem a company is looking to solve.

“If you decentralise something you need a good reason because it slows things down. It adds complexity in a way,” said Eitan Jankelewitz, a partner at Sheridans, a London-based law firm. “I think if you have a genuine use case for decentralisation, blockchain is excellent.” 

A closed blockchain within an organisation could also make sense to address specific data problems.

“It could be [useful] because you're all in the same company, but you have a 100,000-person company with thousands of different database systems and you need to build an accounting and reconciliation system that bridges across silos that are not currently well reconciled,” said Zargham.

But even within a company or consortium of companies, it may be hard sometimes to see the difference between a distributed ledger and its subset distinction of a blockchain. “Especially where you have high trust and aligned interests, you don't need blockchain,” said Bedford. “You can actually run a normal distributed ledger.”

Ultimately, the blockchain’s definition lies on a continuum of technologies; it forks in various directions depending on the exact application. Purists may have their own strict requirements, but with the lack of industry standards, pinpointing an exact definition can be challenging.

Reinventing the blockchain

The fragmented blockchain landscape shows little sign of consolidating without industry standards, especially as businesses continue to experiment with the technology.

“Fundamentally I would expect every company will have a whole bunch of internal blockchains,” said David Yermack, chair of the finance department at New York University. “So there wont be hundreds or even thousands but probably millions in the same way that there are millions of accounting ledgers.” 

Radix is hoping to be the foundational technology behind those ledgers. Even as they argue their product is not a blockchain, the company is targeting problems others say blockchains can solve, like handling data from the likely influx of internet-of-things devices. 

Their protocol relies on a design called sharding, in which nodes contain only a piece of the entire ledger and communicate with other nodes to verify transactions. The process makes the system “linear scalable,” according to Olsen, meaning that transaction volume increases with each node added to the system.

Those defining distributed ledger technologies with a broad stroke may still consider Radix a blockchain. 

But the exact definition may be less important than the story behind it and how companies intend to use the technology.

Radix’s narrative is one of reinvention, but their pitch may not work for all companies looking to adopt the technology, or all developers working in the space.

One developer said in an email the “project is a scam at best”, referring to the company’s claims as “smoke and mirrors” and its founder as a fraud.

“Disregard any project that makes scalability claims without being live for at least 1+ years with real usage,” the developer added.

Olsen said the criticism has been constant over the years and compared the skepticism with their founder, Dan Hughes, with that held for Ethereum’s founder, Vitalik Buterin. “There's nothing I know about that would make me question his authenticity,” Olsen said.

Radix launches its alpha product in June before it opens publicly by the end of the year. By then, the narrative driving blockchain development and how companies make use of the technology may have changed dramatically. 

The key, according to Bedford’s explanation of Deutsche Bank’s strategy, is to stay on top of the developments even if you’re not the ones leading the field. 

In a quickly changing jungle, it’s about not getting left behind.
