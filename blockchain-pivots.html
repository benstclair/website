Companies show mixed success after pivoting to blockchain

Name changes and blockchain acquisitions have proved hit or miss for companies and their shareholders

No company wants to be left behind by new technology. So with the rise of blockchain and cryptocurrencies, some businesses were presented with an opportunity they found too good to resist.

Since the two words entered the public’s lexicon last year, a number of companies have looked to attach blockchain to their work, regardless of whether or not they previously used it — or even dealt with digital technology at all.

In a space often likened to a wild west environment, some of these companies have chosen to navigate the blockchain and cryptocurrencies landscape while already beholden to regulatory filings and shareholder pressure, unlike their startup peers that work more independently.

The companies have done so with mixed success. All 10 of the public companies examined for this report saw their shares rise after announcing they would change their name and pivot to blockchain. Six maintained a higher share price after 120 days of trading, and the others saw their shares decline, sometimes dramatically.

(The analysis did not take into account fluctuations in outstanding shares, which could have diluted or inflated each share’s value.)

Online Blockchain experienced one of the more successful pivots. The UK-based company, formerly known as Online Plc, saw its shares rise and fall sharply before reaching a peak 50 days after the company changed names in October.

Shortly after, the company increased its shares by 13 percent, raising £1 million in the process, diluting its current shareholders and possibly leading to a fall in share price.

With its creation of cryptocurrencies PlusOneCoin and Brazio, a coin specific to the Brazilian market, the company may be exposed to the rise and fall of the general crypto market.

The exposure would match the company’s assertions that its name change reflects a legitimate business pivot, which they had taken well before announcing the change.

“OBC is bulding [sic] a company on strong fundamentals to develop businesses based on Blockchain,” a representative for the company said in an email. “Their focus is on user cases of blockchain applications not riding on hype of crypto or technology.”

Another UK firm, Blockchain Worldwide, formerly Stapleton Capital, and Vancouver-based Hive Blockchain Technologies, formerly Leeta Gold Corp, have each maintained high share prices since their announced pivot. Hive’s share price reached nearly 10 times its previous price after 120 days of trading.

To the market at least, six of the 10 companies examined have made the pivot successfully.

Unsuccessful pivots

While companies pivot operations for a number of reasons, doing so during one of the largest bull runs in history can pose challenges.

Using blockchain successfully requires matching the variations in the technology’s design to its existing architecture, said Anja Bedford, who helps lead Deutsche Bank’s blockchain efforts. A hard pivot without laying previous groundwork may take time to establish shareholder value, or any value at all.

Take Nodechain, for example. Founded in 2013 and formerly known as Alpine 2 Inc, the company changed its name in 2014 to Vapetek Inc before pivoting to blockchain and changing its name again in December. Currently a two-person company, Nodechain holds a market cap just over $2 million.

Their blockchain bump wasn’t immediate, as their shares remained flat until later in December when the company disclosed its purchase of five computers equipped for mining cryptocurrency. In the same announcement, Vapetek said it had ceased operating its e-cigarette and vapour business and planned to sell all related assets.

Over the next two days, the California-based company saw its shares increase 300 percent. But within three months, the price had dropped to less than a third of what it had been worth before the name change, settling 120 trading days later at two percent of its former value.

In the company’s annual report, made public in April, its independent auditor wrote that Nodechain needed additional financing and a sustainable and profitable businesses model to continue operations into the future.

“The ability to successfully resolve these factors raise substantial doubt about the Company’s ability to continue as a going concern,” the auditor concluded.

Nodechain did not respond to multiple requests for comment.

Long Island Iced Tea met a similar fate. A day after changing its name to Long Blockchain in December, shares for the sugary beverage company rose 270 percent.

“We view advances in blockchain technology as a once-in-a-generation opportunity, and have made the decision to pivot our business strategy in order to pursue opportunities in this evolving industry,” then chief executive Philip Thomas said in a statement at the time.

The company said it was in the initial stages of “evaluating specific opportunities involving blockchain technologies”.

But from there the company’s position trended downward: it replaced Thomas in February and announced it would spin off its beverage business. In April NASDAQ suspended the company’s shares from trading on the exchange because of Long’s failure to maintain the minimum market capitalisation.

(Long had previously received suspension notices months before it changed its name, leading some to speculate that their shift to blockchain was an attempt to boost their shares.)

The company’s shares moved to over the counter markets and their price continued to decline, falling to roughly 15 per cent of their value before the company changed names.

Long Blockchain, too, did not respond to multiple requests for comment.
