Stabilised but broken

Repairing NYC’s apartments goes deeper than removing rent stabilisation

Note: The following piece is a condensed version of my undergraduate thesis on the connection bewteen maintenance conditions and New York City’s rent-regulation system. The thesis was 7,400 words and included over 25 interviews with tenants, owners and members of community organisations. I’m including charts produced with SPSS to show the scope of the piece’s statistical analyses. I consider the work a combined piece of long-form journalism and academic research, and my goal was to tell a story both centred on data and grounded in the lived experiences of those effected.

The building’s residents started trickling into the meeting around 7 p.m. For at least one person, who brought an orange plastic stool to sit on, the meeting was nothing new. The rest of us stood around the building’s lobby or sat on the stairs at either side. For the next hour and a half, the residents shared their stories about the building’s maintenance: inconsistent heat and hot water, mould in the bathroom, and a superintendent and landlord who wouldn’t make repairs. 

It was late October 2016, and we were in an apartment building in the South Bronx, several blocks from Yankee Stadium. Back in March, residents had filed a lawsuit against the landlord, citing 238 open violations issued by the city’s housing agency and detailing maintenance issues in 38 apartments — from holes in floors to exposed electrical wires.

The building’s issues were not unique. In 2014, nearly one in five apartments, or over 325,000 units in New York City, experienced three or more maintenance deficiencies. Over 78,000 of these apartments had five or more issues. 

While this building’s residents blamed their superintendent and landlord for the poor maintenance, economists have come to a different conclusion about housing quality in cities like New York. In apartments and buildings like the one I visited, economists point to the large numbers of units subject to rent stabilisation, a distinction that limits the amount landlords can charge each month in rent. 

The narrative goes like this: Without making money from rent increases, landlords do not have the ability or the incentive to make repairs and improvements, so buildings with rent-regulated units fall into disrepair.

With almost all of its apartments under rent stabilisation, the building I visited could serve as an example for what economists have argued.

But according to a statistical model I developed with U.S. Census Bureau data, rent stabilisation is only part of the story. Factors like race and location play a more important role.

In fact, their combined effect on poor maintenance is nearly 70 percent higher than the effect of rent stabilisation alone. Living in that neighbourhood by Yankee Stadium has the highest impact on poor conditions today, 17 percent greater than the impact of stabilised rents.

My finding may not be surprising. Historically speaking, “your apartment is more likely to be in poor living condition if you are black or Latino below a certain income,” said Gregory Jost, former deputy director of the Bronx-based non-profit University Neighborhood Housing Program.

The exact causal mechanisms of location remain unclear, though, and the data leave much unexplained. But the numbers suggest that while it may have been accurate to blame rent stabilisation for the city’s poor maintenance 30 years ago, similar arguments today make increasingly less sense, especially when they’re compared to explanations of race and location. 

A month after I visited the South Bronx apartment, a resident called me and said the conditions in her apartment had not improved. I asked if she had thought about moving. “I’m trying,” she told me. “I’m working two jobs just to get out of here.”
