Are US PUBLIC pensions really going bust?

By Ben St. Clair

When Illinois experienced fiscal challenges in recent years, commentators and lawmakers echoed a common refrain. The state pension plans were continuing to weigh on public finances and needed reform, they said.

The argument, and the controversy surrounding pension plans, is not unique to Illinois.

“For some states, pensions continue to be a source of budget and credit pressure and usually reflect a history of poor funding discipline,” S&P Global wrote in a 2016 report.

Any discussion of public pensions is bound to draw the attention of the 44 million Americans paying into state and local plans across the country, the 14 million currently receiving benefits, and the numerous private firms managing some of the funds’ combined $4.6trn of assents.

But the story commonly told about the demise and sorry state of public pensions may be overblown — and the truth more nuanced than critics make it seem.

“The narrative of runaway pension obligations sinking an ailing city’s finances is simply not supported by the facts,” writes Tom Sgouros of the Haas Institute for a Fair and Inclusive Society.

So what’s actually going on? The answer lies in a mixture of funding and accounting.

Running on a shoestring?

Pension plan liabilities refer to benefits plans expect to pay when members retire. Funds are built up through a combination of government and member contributions and returns on financial investments.

Both maintaining contributions and realising high enough returns to cover benefits can pose challenges for states.

“Adequately funding pension benefits is a challenge for many or most plans,” said Keith Brainard, research director for the National Association of State Retirement Administrators, in a phone interview.

According to Eugene Steuerle, a fellow at the DC-based Urban Institute, politics are partially to blame.

“States are often driven by the same politics as applies to almost all governments: spend now, tax less now, and push some of the burden off to future elected officials and citizens,” he said in an email. “Some give into the temptation more than others.”

States can contribute less to plans if expected investment returns are high and the funding ratio, the gap between current assets and future liabilities, remains low.

Calculating the funding ratio requires making a number of assumptions about future investment returns and the pace at which plans will have to pay retirees. If, in 25 years, a plan expects to pay out $1bn and accounts for a 7.4% annual return, the typical expectation held over the last 16 years by US pension funds, it would need just under $170,000 in assets today to be considered fully funded. If plans are less than “fully funded,” state governments may feel the pressure to make adjustments.

“This practice obscures the true extent of public sector liabilities,” writes Joshua Rauh of the Hoover Institution, a conservative think tank. “The targeted returns may or may not be achieved, but public sector accounting and budgeting proceed under the assumption that they will be achieved with certainty.”

In order to reach high returns (which have mostly failed to reach since 2001), states and cities have turned to alternative investments like private equity, hedge funds, commodities and real estate. The median plan has roughly doubled its exposure to these assets over the last decade, according to an analysis of the Public Plans Database (PPD), which includes data for 170 state and city plans.

Some plans remain more exposed than others. In 2016 the Missouri State Employees pension fund held 62 percent of its $8.8bn assets in alternatives. Georgia’s employee pensions system, on the other hand, held less than one percent of its $29bn assets, among the lowest of pension plans examined.

Alternatives pose a greater risk than bonds, which comprised a large portion of pension funds in the 20th century, but accounting standards allow funds to use expected returns as if they’re guaranteed. The result, Rauh argues, is a funding gap inflated by as much as 170 percent, from $1.4trn to $3.8trn.

“The typical US public pension is run on a shoestring,” write analysts from McKinsey & Company, citing the lack of adequate staffing for the size of the assets under management. With median funding ratios covering roughly three fourths of liabilities in 2016, some may apply the same description to the funds potential to meet future payments.

Like a concentration in alternatives, plan funding ratios vary widely — even within states. But it’s this ratio that critics use to point to “underfunded” plans and a dire future for state retirees.

Thinking differently about pension funding

Other analysts question this line of thinking, however, pointing to the funding ratio’s roots in accounting.

“Although the funding ratio is the most popular and recognised metric of a pension plan’s condition, it is not by itself a reliable indicator of the plan’s condition,” Alex Brown, a researcher with NASRA, said in an email. “Rather, the funding ratio is a ‘point in time’ measurement of a pension plan’s progress toward meeting is long-term funding objectives.”

According to Sgouros, using the ratio to judge a plan’s health fundamentally mistakes the nature of pension liabilities because it assumes that funds will have to immediately make payments to all beneficiaries — an unlikely occurrence.

“Accounting rules imply an equal urgency to all debts, something that is obviously not the case,” Sgouros writes. The only debts that are truly urgent, he argues, are the benefits to pay out each year.

In fact, “under the right conditions, a pension system can pay benefits indefinitely at funding levels much lower than ‘full’ funding,” Sgouros added. As the data show, the combination of investment income and contributions has outpaced required benefit payments in the last 15 years.

In this view, cries of an impending pension crisis are much overblown. Pensions still require proper funding and professionally managed investing teams, but a requirement to fund fully all future liabilities may actually be what is stressing state finance, opponents of the metric contend.

After all, allocating finite tax revenues involves making choices.

“Few government goals can be considered to trump all others, and diversion of taxes into pension savings is a choice not to fund something else,” Sgouros writes.
